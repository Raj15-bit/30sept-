01

Money stays dear, India's shares take the hit, and India's own savers keep buying

The US 30-year borrowing cost closed at its highest since 2001, foreign funds sold Indian shares at the fastest pace in 4 months, and the Nifty ended its worst September in 8 years.

In short
  • Lending to the US government for 30 years now pays about 5.6% a year, the most in 25 years, so shares everywhere look less attractive.
  • Foreign funds sold about ₹20,000 crore of Indian shares on 29 and 30 September, and Indian funds bought almost all of it.
  • The fall is about the price of money and oil, not a broken Indian economy, because India's service and factory numbers are still growing fast.

The number that moved everything: the US 30-year yield

A bond is a loan to a government or a company. The yield is the yearly return a lender asks for. On 29 September the US 30-year yield closed at 5.59%, the highest close since 2001.

When the safest borrower in the world pays this much, every other borrower must pay more too. Home loans in the US now cost about 7.3% to 7.6% a year, the highest since November 2023. That is how a bond market number reaches houses, companies and share prices.

Late on 30 September, some pressure eased. A US inflation reading came in lower than expected, and a senior US central banker said there is "no need for urgency" to raise rates again. The chance of a rate rise in October, as priced by markets, fell from about 70% to about 34%. Even so, the benchmark yield stayed high, so money is still dear.

Why yields stay high even when inflation looks softer

The softer inflation number was partly a change in how it is measured, not a real fall in prices. The US statistics office changed how it counts fund fees, software and legal services, which cut the core number by up to 0.2 points. Service prices outside housing still rose fast in August.

  • Oil — the war around the Strait of Hormuz keeps oil near $100 and diesel at records; section 03 covers this.
  • Debt — the US owes about $40 trillion and borrows about $2 trillion more each year than it collects, so it keeps selling new bonds.
  • The swing in bets — 9 months ago markets expected about 1 point of rate cuts; now they price about 1.25 points of rises by mid-2027, a swing of about 2.25 points.
  • A buyer of last resort — the US Treasury started buying back its own long bonds from 19 August, yet the 30-year yield has risen about 0.4 points since then.

Views split on what comes next. One view is that the US government has lost control of its long bonds and a sudden jump in yields is possible. The other view is that 5.6% for 30 years is an attractive return and buyers will step in, so yields are near a top. Both sides agree that nothing calms down until oil and inflation do.

The big picture: the economy is not the problem

India's economy is still growing. The trial index of service production rose 13.5% from a year earlier in July. Private spending grew 7.1% in the April to June quarter, and passenger vehicle sales rose 21%.

  • Services — 17 of 19 service areas grew in July; retail up 18.5%, real estate up 14.4%, banking up 12.3% and IT services up 10.7% in July; only air transport fell, for the 4th month.
  • Exports — smartphones overtook diesel as India's largest export; section 06 covers this.
  • Prices — consumer inflation averaged 4.2% from April to August, up from 2.5% a year earlier; Citi sees September near 5.6%.
  • Rain — the monsoon ended about 12.6% below normal, the biggest shortfall since 2015; section 08 covers what this means for food prices.

So Indian shares are falling for outside reasons: dear money, dear oil and a weak rupee. One view holds that the market ran ahead of profits in 2023 and 2024 and is now catching down. Another view says the fall is nearly done because Indian funds keep buying and foreign funds are already heavily short. The table in section 10 sets out both sides.

The thread through this issue is simple. Money costs more everywhere, so only businesses with fast-growing, real profits hold their value. That is why India's mid-sized and small companies with rising orders did far better than big, slow-growing names, and why the AI boom, the power build-out and the pharma supply shortage are the themes investors still pay for.

02

Inside India's market: small shares beat big ones, and the deal machine keeps running

The Nifty is flat over 6 months while small and very small companies rose 24% to 38%, BSE joined the Nifty 50, and funds swapped thousands of crores of shares in large blocks.

In short
  • Big companies went nowhere for 6 months, while small companies with rising profits kept climbing.
  • Large blocks of shares moved from foreign sellers and promoters to Indian mutual funds on both days.
  • New share sales are still arriving in large numbers, but some new listings are opening below their issue price.

Over the last 6 months the Nifty barely moved, while indices of small and very small companies rose 24% to 38%. So the fall that hurts headline numbers is mostly in the biggest companies.

The equal-weight Nifty, which gives each of the 50 companies the same weight, now stands at about 1.35 times the normal Nifty, back at its 2018 peak, so the average company is doing far better than the few giants.

  • From the top — the Nifty 50 is about 14% below its all-time high, the Midcap 150 about 7% and the Smallcap 250 only about 4%.
  • Big names — several large companies are down 30% to 50% from their highs; Reliance Industries is down about 24.5% this year and at a 52-week low.

  • IT — after Wipro left the Nifty 50, IT companies have their smallest weight in the index since 2013.

Views split sharply. One view is that small companies are in a real bull market that began in April, and large ones will follow once the Nifty bottoms near 21,500. Futures and options: fewer trades, bigger bets

Trading in Nifty futures in September was the lowest in a month since June 2009. Yet the money paid for index options rose to ₹93,855 crore on 1 day, the highest since 7 April. So fewer people are trading, but those who do are placing larger bets on sharp moves.

  • Rollovers — fewer futures were rolled into October than the 3-month average, a sign of low confidence.
  • Range — traders see the Nifty between 22,500 and 23,000 for now, and would only turn buyers above 23,100 to 23,200; Bank Nifty support is near 54,000.
  • October triggers — the RBI rate decision, results from 8 October starting with TCS, the festive season and a possible Jio listing.

Low volume after a long fall often comes near a bottom, as sellers tire, but it can also mean buyers have stepped away.

03

Oil is winning the shipping battle but not the price battle, and diesel is the real emergency

Gulf oil exports are back near last year's level, yet Brent stays above $100, diesel costs 70% more than before the war, and US states are declaring disasters over fuel.

In short
  • More oil is getting out of the Gulf than at any time since the war began in February, but traders still fear a new attack.
  • Diesel is the tightest fuel in the world, because many refineries are damaged or shut, and it moves trucks, farms and ships.
  • For India this means dear imports, a weak rupee and a heavier use of coal, and a US sanctions law on Russian oil adds a new risk in October.

Oil flows are coming back

The Strait of Hormuz is the narrow sea lane between Iran and Oman that carried about 20% of the world's oil before the war. Over the 7 days to 28 September, flows through it averaged about 83% of the pre-war level.

The big reason is Saudi Arabia. Its East-West pipeline to the Red Sea port of Yanbu was hit by drones on 10 September and is running again at about 3.5 million barrels a day, half its capacity. Loadings at Ras Tanura, its main Gulf port, jumped to about 6.5 million barrels a day from 1.5 million early in the month.

  • Gulf exports — crude exports from Saudi Arabia, Iraq, the UAE and others rose to almost 13 million barrels a day, the most since February, against nearly 19 million before the war.
  • Iran — a US blockade of Iranian ports since July has cut Iranian oil stored outside it to about 15 million barrels from 29 million; it could run out by mid-October.
  • Attacks — a crude tanker was struck on its side by an unknown object near Hormuz on 29 September, 1 of 3 tanker incidents that day, and a ship on the southern route was hit by a drone.

So why is oil still above $100?

Brent crude traded between about $102 and $106 a barrel, falling on news of peace talks hosted by Qatar and then rising again. US crude (WTI) dipped below $90.

The answer from the oil market is fear, not shortage. The US is winning the battle to move ships, but traders fear Iran could strike again, especially after US midterm elections in November. There are also signs in the options market that big investors use oil as insurance against wider market trouble, which keeps prices high even as ships move.

  • Talks — Iran confirmed it received a formal US counter-offer through Qatar; the main fight is over the order of steps, and a 7-day trust-building plan is on the table.
  • Stuck points — Iran wants the US blockade lifted first; the US wants nuclear steps first; reports say combat could resume after the midterms.
  • Qatar — says its gas exports through Hormuz are "barely happening", it has lost about $24 billion of gas sales, and the IMF sees its economy shrinking more than 8% this year.

Diesel is the real emergency

Diesel is the fuel of trucks, tractors, trains and ships. The "crack spread" is the gap between the price of crude and the price of a refined fuel made from it. The diesel crack jumped to about $109 a barrel on 30 September, up almost 9% in a day.

What it means for India

India imports most of its oil, so dear fuel shows up in the trade gap, the rupee and inflation. The poor monsoon has also cut hydropower, so India is leaning harder on coal. Coal stocks at power plants are at a 5-year low measured in days of use, and emergency rules now tell coal plants to run.

  • Captive power — the government asked 112 captive power plants owned by companies such as Vedanta, Tata Steel, Hindalco and JSW to run at full output from October, which could add 5 to 7 GW.
  • Peak demand — expected to rise by 15 to 20 GW, so the grid needs every plant it has.
  • Coal India — beat the market in September on strong power demand and high prices at its e-auctions (spot sales); Jefferies is positive.
  • New supply — about 13 million barrels of Venezuelan crude are heading to India, and Uruguay is building soybean and sunflower oil capacity for India.

The coal point needs care. Coal gives about 70% of India's power and is expected to fall to about 60% by the end of the decade. Coal is also about 48% of Indian Railways' freight, so a shift away from coal would hit railway income, a risk section 06 returns to.

The October risk: US sanctions on Russian oil

Earlier in September the US signed into law a bill co-written by the late Senator Lindsey Graham that allows severe sanctions on buyers of Russian oil. Some investors see 8 to 18 October as the window when India could be hit by tariffs linked to it. India must choose to fall in line, seek a waiver or refuse.

  • Call readouts — Prime Minister Modi's post after a "productive" call with President Trump on trade, defence, energy and technology did not mention the Russian sanctions law, unlike the readouts of other ministerial meetings.
  • Relief talk — President Trump is also reported to be weighing sanctions relief for Russia in exchange for the release of political prisoners, which could soften the law's use.
  • Who is exposed — telecom cable makers HFCL and STL are cited as the Indian shares that would rise sharply if the Russia tariff risk went away.

One view holds that relief for Indian shares needs Brent below $100 and the US 10-year yield below 5% at the same time. Neither test is passed yet, so rallies should be treated as fragile.

04

The world's money markets: a calm index on top, cracks underneath

The S&P 500 sits near its record while most US shares, junk bonds and gold are under strain, and the Fed may still raise rates once more.

In short
  • A handful of giant US companies hold the S&P 500 up while more than half of all US shares are falling.
  • Stress is showing first in the bond market, in low-rated company debt and in the debt of firms borrowing heavily for AI.
  • The US economy is still growing well, which is why the Fed may raise rates again rather than cut them.

US inflation: softer on paper, still hot underneath

The PCE price index is the US Fed's preferred measure of inflation. August's core reading, which leaves out food and fuel, rose 3.0% from a year earlier, below the 3.3% expected. That drove the biggest fall in rate-rise bets of the 2 days.

  • Method change — about 0.2 points of the "miss" came from new ways of counting fund fees, software and legal services, revised back to 2021.
  • Supercore — service prices outside housing rose 0.36% in 1 month, a fast pace; "other services" such as phone plans jumped a record 0.9%.
  • Savings — the savings rate fell to 4.1% from 4.6%, the lowest since November 2022, so families are spending by drawing down savings.
  • Growth — spending rose 0.9% in the month, and the final US growth figure for April to June was 2.2% a year, above the 1.5% expected.
  • Jobs — private payrolls (ADP) rose 90,000 against 70,000 expected; job openings fell to 7.08 million, a 5-month low.
  • Mood — consumer confidence fell to 81.9, the lowest since 2014; the 6-month outlook is 63.6, and below 80 has often come before recessions.

So the data point both ways. John Williams, head of the New York Fed, said there is "no need for urgency" but 1 more rise "late this year" may be right, and Michael Barr said further rises are likely. Since 1990 the Fed has never raised rates at an October meeting just before an election, so bets shifted toward December.

Lending to the US government for 20 or 30 years now pays more than 5.5% a year, which matters because home loans and company bonds are priced off these numbers.

  • Buybacks failed — the US Treasury began buying back long bonds on 19 August; the 30-year yield is about 0.4 points higher since then.
  • Hedge funds — are cutting bets that markets stay calm and pulling back from the Treasury "basis trade" (borrowing to profit from tiny gaps between bond and futures prices), moving the cash into short-term Treasury bills.
  • Cayman buyers — hedge funds in the Cayman Islands hold about $210 billion of bills, the most ever; official data count them as "foreign demand".

Views differ. One view warns that US bonds, the dollar and big government programmes are a bubble that could snap, with loan defaults back at mid-2007 levels. Another says bonds are attractive at these yields, and traders betting on falling prices may be forced to buy back. A further view calls the Fed's rate rises a policy error, since most US shares are already in a bear market (down 20% or more).

The main US index has barely moved for 5 weeks and sits near its record, but the median share fell about 5% over the month, and the equal-weight version is doing worse than at any time since 2002.

The real limits: chips, substrates and power

AI growth now depends on physical supply. China is closing the software gap: DeepSeek released code that runs maths on Huawei's Ascend chips at up to 99.8% of their limit. But Huawei's newest chip output is capped at a few hundred thousand units by a memory shortage, so the supply gap remains.

  • Nvidia — revenue was $96.2 billion in its latest quarter, up 106%, and it guides to $108 billion next quarter.

  • ABF substrate — the base board a big chip sits on; makers such as AT&S (Austria) and Ibiden (Japan) cannot keep up, and 2028 may be the tightest year.
  • Glass cores — the next step; SKC's Absolics plans mass output near end-2026, Samsung Electro-Mechanics in late 2027, Intel near 2030.

Power is the other limit, and the heads of the biggest AI firms now talk about it in terms of national output.

  • Elon Musk — each extra 5 GW is about 1% of US power and could add about 1% to GDP.
  • Jensen Huang — each GW of AI capacity can produce $40 to 50 billion of output a year.

Where India fits: the "anti-AI" market

Goldman Sachs calls India the default "anti-AI" trade. Only about 16% of the MSCI India index is in AI-linked shares, against about 83% in Taiwan and 75% in Korea, so India has missed the AI rally.

That cuts both ways. When money chases AI, India loses flows, part of the foreign selling in section 01; if the AI boom stumbles, India could be a place to hide. One view is to hold US AI shares rather than Indian ones for now; the other is that a global AI setback would favour India.

Insurance: the fight over commissions

IRDAI, India's insurance regulator, wants to cut and cap the expenses insurers can charge, mainly the commissions paid to banks, agents and online sellers. Words explained

Every term used above, in 1 sentence each.

ABF substrateThe thin base board a large chip sits on, which links the chip to the rest of the computer; it is now in short supply for AI chips.
ADP payrollsA monthly count of new private-sector US jobs published by the payroll firm ADP.
AI agentAn AI program that carries out tasks on its own, such as booking, coding or filing, rather than only answering questions.
ALMMIndia's approved list of solar models and makers; government-backed projects must buy from it.
Anti-dumping dutyAn extra import tax on goods sold into a country below their fair price at home.
API (active pharmaceutical ingredient)The chemical in a medicine that does the actual work.
ARR (annual recurring revenue)The yearly revenue implied by a company's latest month of subscriptions or usage.
BarrelAbout 159 litres of crude oil; oil prices are quoted per barrel.
Basis pointOne-hundredth of 1 percentage point, so 100 basis points equal 1 point.
Basis tradeA hedge fund bet that profits from tiny price gaps between government bonds and bond futures, using a lot of borrowed money.
Battery-as-a-serviceA plan where you buy an electric scooter without the battery and rent the battery monthly, which lowers the upfront price.
Bear marketA fall of 20% or more from a recent high.
BESSBattery energy storage system: large batteries that store power, often from solar, for use later.
Block dealA large trade of shares between 2 big investors done in one go, usually in a special trading window.
BondA loan given to a government or company that pays interest and is repaid on a set date.
BrentThe main world price for crude oil, based on North Sea oil.
Bulk dealA trade in which 1 investor buys or sells more than 0.5% of a company's shares in a day.
BushingAn insulated part that carries high-voltage current safely through a transformer's metal casing.
CAFE normsFuel-use and CO2 limits that apply to the average of all cars a maker sells in a year.
CAPE (Shiller)Share price divided by average profit over 10 years, adjusted for inflation; a long-term measure of how dear shares are.
Captive power plantA power plant a company owns to supply its own factories.
CCA (collaborative combat aircraft)An unmanned combat jet that flies alongside a piloted fighter; also called a robot wingman.
CDMOA contract development and manufacturing organisation, which develops and makes drugs for other companies.
CDS (credit default swap)Insurance against a borrower failing to repay; a higher CDS price means more worry about that borrower.
Closing auction session (CAS)A short window at the end of trading where the final price is set by matching all orders at once.
Core inflationPrice rises leaving out food and fuel, which jump around a lot.
Crack spreadThe gap between the price of crude oil and the price of a fuel made from it, such as diesel; it shows how much refiners earn.
CTAA trend-following fund that buys what is rising and sells what is falling, often by computer.
DGTRIndia's trade remedies office, which recommends anti-dumping and other protective duties.
DIIDomestic institutional investor: Indian mutual funds, insurers, banks and pension funds.
DPCOIndia's Drug Price Control Order, which sets price caps on medicines on a national list.
Dyed dieselDiesel coloured red that is taxed less and normally allowed only in farm and off-road vehicles.
E-auction (coal)Coal sold by Coal India through online bidding at market prices, rather than under fixed contracts.
El NiñoA warming of the Pacific Ocean that often weakens India's monsoon rain.
EPCEngineering, procurement and construction: a contract to design, buy parts for and build a project.
Equal-weight indexAn index that gives every company the same weight, instead of weighting by company size.
Exchange (oil reserve)A loan of oil from the government reserve that companies must return later, with extra barrels as interest.
Existential riskA risk that could end or permanently cripple human civilisation.
F&OFutures and options: contracts that let traders bet on or protect against future price moves.
Fed (Federal Reserve)America's central bank, which sets the main US interest rate.
FIIForeign institutional investor: a foreign fund that buys listed Indian shares or bonds.
FuturesA contract to buy or sell something at a set price on a future date.
Gigawatt (GW)1,000 megawatts; roughly the output of 1 large power plant.
GLP-1 drugsA class of medicines, such as semaglutide, used for diabetes and weight loss.
Green Energy CorridorIndia's programme to build power lines that carry wind and solar power from where it is made to where it is used.
Hallmark (HUID)A stamp and 6-character code showing the purity of gold jewellery.
Heat mapA grid of tiles coloured by how much each item rose or fell; greener means a bigger rise, redder a bigger fall.
High yield (junk) bondA bond from a borrower with a low credit rating, which pays more interest because the risk of default is higher.
